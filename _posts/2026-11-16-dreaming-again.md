# Dreaming Again

My life changed in April 2025. My dream to be an aerospace engineer at NASA had come true. I was hired as a support service contractor working on a spaceflight program. I have the title of aerospace engineer. I am doing the work of an aerospace engineer. I am doing my work at NASA. I’m even helping make key decisions on spacecraft and mission design. This checks all the boxes, right? For some reason that green stripe on the contractor badges made me feel like there was one more step. I worked at NASA, but I didn’t work _for_ NASA.

For many, that really is a meaningless distinction, and correctly so. The work is really no different between contractor and civil servant at my level. The only difference is the pay and benefits, and even those are comparable at this point in my career. Nobody looks down on the contractors or treats them any differently. The civil servants might get first go at signing up for trainings, but I’ve been able to take a class or two without issue. Why do I feel like being a contractor isn’t enough? How do I get that green stripe off my badge?

Get lucky with timing to be here for one of the largest paradigm shifts in the history of the agency.

When Jared Isaacman was named NASA administrator, one of the first workforce directives was to bring engineering talent back “in-house” rather than depending on contractors. He called it Restoring NASA’s Core Competencies. If there are people who have been supporting NASA for a while and will continue to, why not remove the overhead and make them a government employee? That was the plan. It sounded like a great opportunity for me, but my worry was that I hadn’t been around long enough or shown that I am competent enough to be someone that the agency wanted to convert.

_What a silly worry._

Or was it? The posting to apply opened in early August. Weeks went by. Nothing. Then the social media posts started popping up of NASA Glenn conversions. What about me? Did I get skipped? Was I right to be concerned? No, it was just the strategy that the center leadership took. They converted smaller contracts before focusing on the bigger ones, and the contract that I am a part of is one of the two biggest. Then I got some inside information that it was not a matter of if, but when.

**I got the offer.**

The email came at 10:00 PM, which was quite unexpected. Getting an offer at all should not have been a surprise, however. Everyone that I work with tells me how good I have been doing and how vital I am to the mission, but I felt like I needed a new badge and to not have the contractor name listed by my name before I felt like I belonged. It’s a little irrational, but I feel like I would only be legitimate if I was a true NASA employee. Ridiculous, I know. One of the managers here put it in a way that might capture my feelings about it when he congratulated me on getting the offer to convert. He said that I am now a more permanent part of the NASA family.

I wanted to be a NASA rocket scientist for as long as I can remember. I don’t even have a reason that I can go back to that sparked it. All I know is that this was the goal, the dream.

My journey to the stars is complete.
